|---|---|---|---|

## Ideal customer profile

Scored out of 10. Anything under 6 is not worth the selling time — with deals this
size and cycles this long, a bad-fit pursuit costs a quarter.

| Signal | Weight | What good looks like |
|---|---|---|
| Buyer type | 3 | Hospital & health service, university health/nursing faculty, state government health or innovation department, medical publisher |
| Cohort size | 2 | Enough learners to justify the build — repeat annual cohorts beat one-offs |
| Funding identifiable | 2 | A named grant, training budget or innovation fund, not "we'd have to find money" |
| Evidence sensitivity | 2 | They care whether the education *works* and will pay for the evidence base |
| Geography | 1 | Australia, with regional and remote services scoring highest — VR solves a real distance problem there |

### Segments, and how each actually buys

**Hospital & health services.** Educator or Director of Clinical Education is the
champion; money sits with the Executive Director or an innovation fund; procurement
runs through a state panel. Buys to solve a training-access problem, especially
across distributed sites. Regional and remote services have the sharpest version of
the problem and are the strongest fit. Long cycles, high repeat, high reference
value to neighbouring services.

**Universities (health and nursing faculties).** An academic running the unit is the
champion; the Head of School or Dean is the economic buyer; the money is
faculty-held and moves on semester planning cycles. Buys to improve simulation
capacity without more lab hours and staff. They are the segment most responsive to
peer-reviewed evidence.

**State government departments.** Innovation, science or health portfolios. Funds
programs rather than products, often through grants or initiatives with a defined
acquittal deadline. The largest single deals, the least repeatable, and the most
sensitive to machinery-of-government changes.

**Medical publishers.** Buy content and licensing, not delivery. Shortest cycle,
most commercial, most price-sensitive, and the most genuinely scalable of the four.

### Disqualifiers

- No identifiable funding source after two conversations
- Wants the hardware, not the education
- Single cohort with no prospect of repeating
- Requires a full tender with no incumbent relationship, unless the deal is very
  large — the win rate does not justify the effort

## Objections and the answers that have worked

Record the answer that actually moved a deal, not the answer that sounds good.

| Objection | Answer | Worked on |
|---|---|---|
| "We already have simulation facilities" | Position as extending reach to sites and cohorts the lab cannot serve, not as replacing the lab | `<deal>` |
| "Is there evidence VR education works?" | The evidence base and the reporting standard — turn the objection into the differentiator | `<deal>` |
| "We don't have headsets" | Separate the education from the hardware; price them apart so hardware is not a blocker on the education decision | `<deal>` |
| "No budget this year" | Find the window: which budget, when it resets, whether an innovation or grant line applies. Often a timing objection, not a money objection | `<deal>` |
| "Send me something and I'll circulate it" | Offer to present to the group instead; a circulated PDF dies | `<deal>` |
| "We'd need to go to tender" | Establish whether an existing panel or a below-threshold engagement applies before accepting the premise | `<deal>` |

## Email patterns

Every follow-up carries something the recipient did not have before. Four patterns,
in descending order of how well they work in this sector:

1. **Evidence drop** — a finding, publication or comparable deployment relevant to
   the problem they described, with one line on what it means for them.
2. **Named next step** — a specific proposed action with a date attached, so the
   reply can be one word.
3. **Funding-window prompt** — surfacing a real deadline in their own budget cycle.
   Urgency they recognise, not urgency you invented.
4. **Genuine check-out** — after four unanswered touches, a short note saying you
   will stop unless they want to pick it up. This gets a reply more often than the
   three before it.

Never send: "just checking in", "bumping this to the top of your inbox", "circling
back". Health and university buyers read those as noise from a vendor, and they are
correct to.
