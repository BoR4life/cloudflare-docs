# Evidence base

Citable literature for pitches, grant applications, abstracts and public
argument. Australian-relevant and peer-reviewed, not vendor content and
not conference talks.

## Rules for using this file

- **Never invent a citation, a DOI or a figure.** If a number is needed
  and it is not here, go and find it — PubMed is connected — or say the
  number is not established. A fabricated statistic in front of an
  academic partner or a grant assessor ends the relationship.
- **Never import United States care-pathway claims into an Australian
  pitch.** US material frequently states that a new mother has no
  scheduled contact for weeks after birth. That is a US artefact.
  Australia has domiciliary midwifery visits in the early postnatal
  period across most states and, in Victoria, a universal free Maternal
  and Child Health service with a scheduled visit series. Repeat the US
  claim to a Victorian maternity executive and the room is lost — they
  run that service. The defensible Australian claim is narrower and
  truer: contact is scheduled, brief, and weighted toward the baby, and
  it is not there at 2am on day nine.
- **Distinguish rhetoric from evidence.** A vivid figure from a public
  talk may be used to open a LinkedIn post. It may not be used in a
  grant, an abstract, or a claim to a health service.

## The core paper — why the education layer does not happen

Talbot H, Peters S, Furber C, Smith DM. *Midwives' experiences of
discussing health behaviour change within routine maternity care: A
qualitative systematic review and meta-synthesis.* Women and Birth.
2024;37:303–316. doi:10.1016/j.wombi.2024.01.002

Twenty-two qualitative studies, eight countries, 2005–2023. Published by
Elsevier on behalf of the Australian College of Midwives, open access
under CC BY.

The overarching finding is the Kindred Care thesis stated by the sector
itself: **midwives recognised the importance of health behaviour change
discussions, but these conversations were not prioritised in clinical
practice.** The paper's three barrier sub-themes are time constraints and
competing tasks; lack of knowledge, skills and confidence; and lack of
resources and multidisciplinary support.

Two quotes from midwives in the included studies carry the argument
better than any product claim:

> "Time's the biggest thing really… they could have been booked in for a
> 15 min slot instead of a 30 min slot, so things like that [health
> promotion], that's the first thing that goes out of the window."

> "We've got to tick boxes, we've got to tick that we've discussed
> alcohol, we've discussed smoking."

How to use it. This is the evidence that the education layer is
systematically the first thing dropped when clinical time is short — not
because midwives don't value it, but because the system doesn't protect
it. Kindred Care therefore does not replace a conversation that is
happening. It exists because that conversation reliably does not happen.
That framing is respectful of midwives, which matters, because midwives
decide whether this product lives.

The paper's own conclusion calls for intervention development to support
midwives with health behaviour change communication. That is a named
research gap in the ACM's journal that Kindred Care addresses directly —
usable as the justification paragraph in a grant application.

The paper also invokes pregnancy as a recognised **Teachable Moment** — a
health or life event that opens a window for behaviour change. That is
pedagogy language, and it is the cleanest available bridge between the
product argument and the founder's doctoral work on AI-assisted adult
learning.

## The paper to pick up before someone throws it

Collins E, Keedle H, Jackson M, Lequertier B, Schmied V, Boyle J, Kildea
S, Dahlen HG. *Telehealth use in maternity care during a pandemic: A lot
of bad, some good and possibility.* Women and Birth. 2024;37:419–427.
doi:10.1016/j.wombi.2023.12.008

Australian, large, and authored by some of the most cited midwifery
researchers in the country. Two data sources from the Birth in the Time
of COVID-19 study: interviews with 20 women and 16 midwives, plus open
text from surveys of 687 midwives and 2525 women.

Findings that cut against any remote maternity offering: women described
**inadequate assessment** and negative impacts on **communication and
rapport**; midwives shared those concerns and reported technological
challenges; delivery was inconsistent nationally with no national
guidelines; and the authors warn telehealth "has the potential to further
reduce equitable, and appropriate care delivery for those at greatest
risk of poor outcomes." Many women were left "feeling let down by the
maternity care system."

The conclusion is the sentence to memorise, because a sceptical clinician
will quote it at you:

> telehealth "may play an adjunct role in post-pandemic maternity
> services, but is not a suitable replacement to traditional
> face-to-face maternity care."

How to use it. Cite it yourself, first, before anyone else does. Read
correctly it is not an objection to Kindred Care — it is a specification
for it. The harms the paper identifies all come from remote delivery
**substituting** for clinical contact and attempting **assessment** at a
distance. Kindred Care does neither: it is education-only,
non-diagnostic, explicitly adjunct, and routes the parent back to her own
care team. Naming this literature unprompted demonstrates that the
product was designed with the sector's own evidence in hand, which is a
stronger credibility signal than any feature list.

One further detail worth deploying: during the pandemic 87.5% of
Australian maternity telehealth was delivered by telephone, with no
national guidelines in place. The remote education layer has never been
properly designed in this country — only improvised under emergency
conditions. That is the gap.

## Loss and bereavement — an evidence vacuum, and the highest-risk content

Hildingsson I, Berterö C, Hultcrantz M, Kärrman Fredriksson M, Peira N,
Silverstein RA, Persson M, Sveen J. *Support interventions to reduce
psychological distress in families experiencing stillbirth in high income
countries: A systematic review.* Women and Birth. 2024;37:296–302.

Databases searched to August 2022. Only four intervention studies met
inclusion (US, UK, Finland, Australia). Results could not be synthesised.
Risk of bias assessed as high in all four; certainty of evidence rated
very low for all outcomes. **No studies of interventions for siblings
were found.**

How to use it. Carefully. It establishes that bereavement support after
stillbirth is a genuine evidence vacuum, which supports the inclusivity
argument that non-default journeys are poorly served. It also means there
is no validated model to build on, and loss is the single highest-risk
content domain in the product. Any claim that Kindred Care supports
bereaved parents must be scoped tightly, co-designed with bereavement
specialists, and never presented as a therapeutic intervention. If in
doubt in a pitch, describe loss as a journey the content model is
designed not to exclude, rather than a feature.

## International and equity framing

Garti I, Gray M, Bromley A, Tan JYB. *Midwives' experiences of providing
pre-eclampsia care in a low- and middle-income country – A qualitative
study.* Women and Birth. 2024;37:332–339.

Australian-authored (Charles Darwin University, Edith Cowan, University
of Southern Queensland), 35 in-depth interviews with Ghanaian midwives.
Midwives are the frontline for obstetric emergency care; among their
recommendations are expanded continuing professional development and
**tailored public education**.

How to use it. Supporting material for the international track and for
the argument that a governed education layer extends scarce midwifery
capacity where it is thinnest. Not for the Australian beachhead pitch —
a Victorian service does not want its maternity system compared to
Ghana's.

## What is still missing

Gaps to close before the GTM, the business case or the PhD can lean on
Australian numbers rather than borrowed ones:

- Australian prevalence for postnatal pelvic floor dysfunction,
  persistent pain, and urinary incontinence after birth.
- Australian data on avoidable postnatal presentations to GPs and
  emergency departments that better-timed education could prevent —
  this underwrites the core service-level value claim and is currently
  asserted rather than evidenced.
- Evidence on maternal confidence and self-efficacy as measurable
  outcomes, to support the evaluation design.
- Australian evidence on equity of postnatal access for CALD, First
  Nations, rural and remote families.
