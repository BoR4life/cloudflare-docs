# Marketing and sales strategy

The operating strategy for a pre-evidence, pre-revenue clinical product
selling into Australian public health services. Written against the real
constraint, not against a generic marketing playbook.

## What is actually being sold

Not the Companion. At this stage nobody is buying software.

The thing being asked for is that a cash-constrained public health
service goes first — on an unproven product, in a category its clinical
governance committee is nervous about, with no published evaluation, from
a company with no reference site. Stated plainly, that is close to
unsellable, and every hour spent polishing product messaging instead of
addressing it is wasted.

**So do not sell the product. Sell participation in the evidence.**

The offer that converts is co-authorship of the thing that does not yet
exist: an evaluation, a publication, a piece of sector-first work with
academic governance attached, in which the service and its clinical
leaders are named participants rather than customers. This costs the
service reputational risk instead of budget, which is the currency it
actually has. It also happens to be true — the evaluation is genuinely
the next milestone — which is why it survives scrutiny.

Every asset, every email, every post should be built to make one
individual clinical leader think *I want my name on that*.

## Why generic 2026 marketing advice does not apply

The standard playbook — AI-driven personalisation, multi-channel
presence, contextual campaigns, funnel optimisation, revenue KPIs —
assumes inbound volume, a media budget, a short sales cycle, and a large
buyer population. None of those hold here.

- **Buyer population is tiny.** A handful of decision-makers per state.
  A campaign that reaches ten thousand people has reached nobody who
  matters. This is named-account selling, not marketing.
- **The sales cycle is measured in years** and gated on evidence and
  procurement, not on nurture sequences.
- **Paid channels contradict the financial model.** The low
  acquisition-cost assumption rests on relationship-led distribution
  through the academic channel. Spending on media undermines the
  investment case it supports.
- **There is nothing to convert to.** No product in market, no
  self-serve, no trial. A demand-generation funnel would generate
  interest that cannot be fulfilled, which is worse than no interest.

Revenue KPIs are the right destination and the wrong instrument now.
Measured today they read as zero and tell you nothing.

## What marketing is for at this stage

Not demand generation. **Credibility manufacturing.** Three plays, in
priority order.

**1. The argument.** A public, repeated, first-person position on two
things: why generative AI in education can make learning worse when it is
designed for fluency over thinking, and why postnatal education fails at
precisely the moment it is needed. Owned by a nurse who has done the
shifts and is doing the doctorate. This is the only marketing asset that
compounds, it costs nothing but time, and it is the reason a clinical
leader takes the first meeting.

**2. The evidence.** Cite the sector's own literature before anyone cites
it at you (see `evidence.md`). Publish. Present. A peer-reviewed abstract
in the right journal is worth more than a year of posting, because it
converts the founder from vendor to colleague in the eyes of the exact
audience that decides.

**3. The room.** Professional colleges, conferences and sector events put
you in front of concentrated audiences of the right clinicians at low
cost. The current work is converting existing goodwill from professional
bodies into structured advocacy — a speaking slot, a special-interest
group, a named advisor — rather than repeated warm noise.

Everything else — website polish, brand systems, social cadence, lead
magnets — is deferred until there is something to convert.

## The sales motion

One motion, run repeatedly, one named account at a time:

1. **Warm route in.** Never cold. The academic midwifery network, the
   founder's own clinical relationships, or an advisor's introduction.
2. **Find the clinical champion**, not the executive. Someone who wants
   it to exist for their own professional reasons.
3. **Arm the champion for governance** before they hit it. Education-only
   scope, non-diagnostic mandate, escalation to the woman's own care
   team, Australian-resident data. Volunteer for the scrutiny.
4. **Offer co-development, not a licence.** Named participation in the
   evaluation, research output, profile.
5. **Get one dated artefact** — a letter of support, a governance
   review, a site visit, an LOI. Written, on their letterhead.
6. **Protect the delivery date.** A slipped pilot costs the reference
   site, which is worth more than the licence.

## The metrics that mean something now

Leading indicators, counted weekly, in this order:

- Warm replies answered within 24 hours (target: all of them).
- Named clinical champions, by service.
- Dated meetings in the next fortnight.
- Written artefacts secured: letters of support, LOIs, governance
  reviews cleared, named pilot sites.
- Speaking slots and accepted abstracts.
- Stage movements, and opportunities with no movement in six weeks.

Lagging indicators to hold at zero without alarm until Stage 2: revenue,
licences, pipeline value. Reporting a weighted pipeline value on
non-revenue LOIs is a probity breach, not a KPI (see `probity.md`).

## Sequencing the four audiences

Do not run all four at once with one founder's time.

1. **Health services** — the revenue engine, the slowest, and the one
   that needs the evidence. Run it now because it takes longest.
2. **Midwifery education** — the fastest yes. Sits on a training budget,
   needs lighter governance, and the academic channel is already there.
   It is the most likely source of a first paid contract and it
   generates the standardised-client evidence that helps the health
   service case. Under-prioritised relative to its yield.
3. **Consumer** — sequenced after B2B volume; unit economics do not work
   before then.
4. **Insurers** — gated on trial signal. Not a current-year activity.

## Where this sits against the rest of the business

Kindred Care cannot be accelerated past clinical governance, academic
validation and procurement. Its slowness is its moat. That makes it a
poor source of near-term cash and a strong source of long-term
defensibility.

The strategic error to avoid is funding a slow institutional bet from a
position of personal financial exposure. Kindred Care needs a cash
engine beside it that requires nobody's committee approval — the
existing education and XR business, and the proven international
channel. Keeping those two separate, and honest about which is which, is
what buys Kindred Care the eighteen months it genuinely needs.
