# Outbound to health services and universities

## The buying unit

Nobody buys this alone. A health-service deal needs four roles, and the
deal stalls at whichever one is missing:

- **Clinical champion** — a midwifery or maternity leader who wants it to
  exist. Without one, nothing starts. This person is usually reached
  through the ACU midwifery network rather than cold.
- **Executive sponsor** — a service or hospital executive who can commit
  budget and put the service's name on a letter. Without one, the pilot
  never becomes a licence.
- **Governance gatekeeper** — clinical governance, digital health, or
  privacy. They cannot say yes, but they can say no, and they will if the
  product looks like software as a medical device or the data leaves
  Australia. Arm the champion for this conversation early; do not wait to
  be ambushed by it.
- **Education or workforce lead** — often the fastest yes, because the
  midwifery-education framing lands on a training budget rather than a
  clinical one, and needs less governance to trial.

When a service goes quiet after a good first meeting, it is almost always
because the champion hit the gatekeeper without support.

## First contact

Structure that works, four short paragraphs:

1. **The ask, immediately.** A 30–60 minute introduction to walk the team
   through the prototype. Not "I'd love to explore synergies".
2. **What it is and who governs it.** One sentence on the Companion, one
   on academic governance, one naming the early support from professional
   bodies. Credibility before capability.
3. **What is in it for them.** Phased partnership, joint funding,
   academic research output, extended clinical impact without added
   workflow friction. Health-service leaders respond to research
   participation and equity reach at least as strongly as to product.
4. **The date question.** Ask what works. Never "let me know if you're
   interested".

Attach one overview document, not three. Cc the person who warmed the
relationship — a warm cc converts far better than a cold to.

## The champion play

Where a colleague or academic partner is making the approach on your
behalf, give them a generic pitch summary they can adapt, and tell them
explicitly what you want the contact positioned as: early adopter and
co-development partner, not customer. Offer the contact something
personal — co-authorship on research outputs, a role in business
development — because in health services the individual's own
professional interest is frequently the thing that actually moves.

Then set a date to ask how it went. Introductions made and never followed
up are the most common leak in this pipeline.

## Follow-up cadence

- **No reply after first contact:** one follow-up at 7 days, with a new
  piece of information rather than a nudge — a relevant development, a
  new supporter, a date you are in their city. A second follow-up at
  ~21 days. Then park it and change the route: go through a person, not
  the inbox.
- **After a good meeting:** written summary within 24 hours with the
  specific next step and who owns it. If they said yes to something, put
  it in writing the same day — that document becomes the thing the
  champion forwards internally.
- **Warm reply asking for a meeting:** answer same day. Always. This is
  the single highest-value action available and it decays fast.

## Objection handling

**"Isn't this just ChatGPT / a chatbot?"** — See `positioning.md`. Answer
with provenance, scope discipline and pedagogy. Never with features.

**"Is this a medical device? What does our clinical governance say?"** —
Meet it head-on before they raise it. Education-only by design,
non-diagnostic by mandate, escalation routes to the parent's own
nominated care team, Australian-resident data. Offer to walk their
governance lead through it directly; volunteering for that scrutiny is
itself the strongest signal.

**"We already have patient education materials."** — Agree. They almost
certainly have good ones, unread, at the moment they are needed. The gap
is not content, it is timing and conversation. The product does not
replace what they have; it delivers it at 3am in the parent's own words.

**"Our staff are already stretched."** — The Companion adds no workflow.
It is a layer between appointments, not inside them. If it works, the
service sees fewer reassurance-seeking contacts, not more.

**"Where's the evidence?"** — Be honest: the evidence is the work
immediately ahead, and that is the offer. Early sites are not buying a
proven product, they are co-authoring the evaluation that makes it
proven, with the academic credit and profile that come with it. Do not
bluff maturity — for the research-minded services worth having, honesty
is the pitch.

**"What does it cost?"** — Never improvise a number. Get the tier
structure from the canonical pricing model, confirm birth volume, and
come back with a written position. If the conversation is at
early-adopter stage, the honest answer is that pilot and co-design
participation is structured differently from a licence and you would
rather scope it properly than quote badly.

**"Who else is using it?"** — Name organisations that have actually
committed to something, at the level they committed. Do not inflate
warmth into commitment; health-service executives talk to each other and
will check.

## What not to do

- Do not send commercial terms or data-room documents to anyone outside
  an NDA.
- Do not name one prospect to another as validation without asking.
- Do not promise a delivery date the programme cannot hold. A missed
  pilot date costs the reference site, which is worth more than the
  licence.
- Do not chase a dead lead because it is on the list. An advisor telling
  you a contact is an administrator rather than an innovator is data —
  reallocate the hours to a warm reply instead.
